INDICATIONS

What this is, in one screen

MOLECULE is a decision simulator wearing a tycoon game. You run a pharmaceutical company across ten years — 120 monthly turns — and the only thing that counts at the end is the value of the stake you still own.

The unit of time

1 click = 1 month

Set your levers, then advance. Ten years is roughly one development cycle — long enough that today's revenue was decided by choices you made years ago.

The unit of progress

Molecule → brand

Revenue exists only after a molecule survives five R&D phases and you build the plant to supply it. Nothing earns before launch.

The unit of winning

Founder stake value

Your score is ownership × share price. Build real value and don't give the company away raising money for it.

§1

Directions — your first 24 months

  1. Register a profile, then found a company. Pick a therapeutic area — you're choosing an economic model, not a disease. Read its one-line note before you commit.
  2. You start with a Phase III asset and an early program. Keep the Phase III moving and open one more program so the board is never empty.
  3. When your first molecule is approved, open the launch plan. Set price near reference (0.9–1.1×) and build capacity to roughly 70% of mature demand. Don't over-build on day one.
  4. The month you launch, start funding all four demand drivers and assign reps to ~50% detailing coverage. Drivers decay if you don't.
  5. Watch cash. Below zero draws the revolver; exhaust the revolver and you're insolvent. The failure mode is almost never a bad molecule — it's a good one funded on a timeline the balance sheet couldn't carry.

DOSAGE

The six screens

What each one is for, and the levers that matter. Every section in the game carries a "?" — tap it in-game for the same explanation in context.

℞ 01

Pipeline — research & development

Programs move through Discovery → Phase I → II → III → Regulatory review. Each phase burns cash monthly and can fail — money spent on a failed program is gone. The blister strip on each program is a live progress bar: filled pockets are cleared phases, the magenta pocket is where you are.

Levers: start new programs; set trial intensity (lean = cheap, slow, riskier · standard · accelerated = expensive, fast, slightly safer); rename a molecule to give it a brand identity; terminate a program to stop the bleed. Only an approved molecule opens a launch plan.

℞ 02

Portfolio — marketed brands

Where revenue lives. Each brand shows net revenue, patients, share, net price, gross-to-net, and capacity use. Four demand drivers decide your prescriptions, and all four decay every month you stop paying for them.

Levers: monthly spend on promotion, medical affairs, and market access; expand manufacturing capacity (comes online next month); reprice. Capacity is a hard ceiling — unserved demand goes to the competitor and doesn't come back.

℞ 03

Field force — commercial operations

Representatives are your only commercial cost that scales linearly and can't be switched off mid-quarter. Assign them across brands to build detailing coverage toward the level each market needs.

Levers: hire/cut headcount; assign reps per brand; fund a compliance programme. Watch your compliance posture — promote too aggressively and an audit becomes a fine.

℞ 04

Market — competitive landscape

Each therapeutic area is a different economic model: patient volume, price ceiling, elasticity, and competitive intensity. Intensity rises every month whether or not you act. The exclusivity clock counts every brand down to loss of exclusivity, when generics collapse your net price.

Read for: where your share sits, which areas are heating up, and how many months of exclusivity each brand has left.

℞ 05

Finance — ledger, capital & stock

The P&L closes every month. Below-zero cash draws the revolver. Once enterprise value clears the threshold you can take the company public — an IPO raises cash but permanently dilutes you. Afterward: a live share price, secondary offerings, and buybacks.

Levers: IPO (choose your float); secondary offering (raise cash, dilute); buyback (spend cash, raise ownership). Your stake value = ownership × price.

℞ 06

Rankings — industry leaderboard

Nine rival firms, each with its own enterprise value, share price, and products. They grow and launch every month. When a rival launches in your area, competitive intensity there rises and your share falls — the table is wired to the simulation, not decoration. You're ranked by market value against them.

MECHANISM · R&D

Five phases, one clock

Every molecule you own is somewhere on this strip. Each phase has a duration, a cost, and a probability of success. Fail, and the pocket cracks — along with everything you spent getting there.

Discovery · 4mo

Phase II · 9mo
Phase III · 13mo

What the phases teach

Attrition is front-loaded and brutal — Phase II is where most molecules die. A program you nurse through three phases and lose in the fourth cost you years and cash for nothing. That's the real R&D lesson: a full pipeline is insurance, not ambition.

Trial intensity is a trade

Lean protocols buy runway at the cost of time and success odds. Accelerated protocols buy speed and a slightly better read-out at a real cash premium. Pick intensity against your cash position and the exclusivity clock, not out of habit.

MECHANISM · COMMERCIAL

Four dials decide your prescriptions

Demand is not a property of the molecule. It's the product of four drivers you pay to build — and every one of them decays the month you stop. A weak molecule can't be rescued by spend; a strong one can be squandered by neglect.

AwarenessPromotion / DTC
DetailingField force coverage
AccessRebates / formulary
CredibilityMedical affairs / KOL
How to read them

The four drivers combine into a prescription share — but they aren't interchangeable. In oncology and rare disease, credibility and access outrun promotion. In cardio and anti-infectives, detailing reach and access carry the volume. Fund the drivers your area actually rewards, and stop over-feeding one you've already saturated.

Decay is the point. A driver at 70% that you stop paying for slides back toward its floor within a few months — which is why a brand you've "finished" launching still needs a maintenance budget, and why walking away from a brand is a decision, not a default.

WARNINGS

Three tensions the game will not let you dodge

Every hard decision in MOLECULE is one of these three in disguise. If a choice feels easy, you've probably missed which tension you're actually resolving.

Access costs price
Buying formulary position raises your gross-to-net. Coverage you can't convert into volume is just margin you gave to a payer. The discipline: only pay for access that moves prescriptions — and prove it does.
Capacity is a bet
You commit manufacturing capacity before you know demand. Under-build and unserved patients go to the competitor permanently; over-build and cash sits in idle plant. There is no safe default — only a calibrated wager.
The clock never stops
Exclusivity is finite and competitive intensity rises every month. A share you are not actively defending is a share you are donating. Standing still is the one move that always loses.

Why the score is your stake, not market cap

A founder who builds a $2B company but floats 80% of it to get there is worth less than one who builds $900M and kept 70%. The game rewards value created minus value given away — the actual job of capital allocation.

When to raise

Raise equity only when the pipeline can turn the cash into more than the dilution costs you. A secondary offering to fund a Phase III that launches is smart; one to plug a hole you dug with idle capacity is how you shrink your own stake.

INTERACTIONS

Events, decisions & saving

Things happen between your moves. Some are opportunities, some are ambushes, and the game judges the cumulative pattern of your choices, not any single one.

Decisions

Safety signals, payer rebate demands, audits, lawsuits, activist investors, M&A offers, supply shocks, tenders. Each is a fork with a cost on both sides — there is rarely a free option.

M&A

You'll be offered clinical-stage assets and marketed brands to buy, and unsolicited offers to sell. Buying skips years of attrition at a premium; selling books cash but surrenders the upside.

Saving

Autosave fires after every action (watch the "Saved" chip). Progress lives in Firebase if you connect it, on your device otherwise, and you can always export a .sav file as a portable backup.

HOW SUPPLIED

Scoring & the ten-year review

At month 120 the board grades you on founder stake value. These bands are your target lines — the challenge dossier is built around them.

CLINICAL CASES

The challenge dossier

Ten cases, three tiers. Each isolates one competency a brand manager or director gets measured on in real life, gives you a measurable win condition calibrated to the actual simulation, and tells you the moves that clear it. Run them as scenarios — reload if you miss. The dose rating is difficulty.

Tier I

Brand Manager

Own one brand. Prove you can launch it, feed it, price it, and defend it.
BM · 01

The Clean Launch

Competency — launch excellence
Dose
In your real worldA launch you only get to do once. Price and supply are anchored in the first weeks and are nearly impossible to walk back — the pre-launch decisions carry the whole curve.
Setup
Standard start$210M. Any area. Take your inherited Phase III asset to approval.
Objective
$8M net / mowithin 18 months of launch — without drawing the revolver.
Constraints
Organic onlyNo IPO, no acquisitions. This is about the launch, not the balance sheet.
Moves to run
  1. Launch at 0.9–1.1× reference price. Premium pricing here starves access before you've earned credibility.
  2. Build capacity to ~70% of mature demand shown in the launch planner — enough to serve the ramp, not so much that cash sits idle.
  3. From month one, fund promotion and access together and assign reps to ~50% detailing coverage. All four drivers start at their floor.
  4. Hold your nerve through the ramp — share compounds as awareness and access climb. Don't reprice in the first year.
The lesson
A launch is price and capacity set before month one, then four drivers fed without a gap. Everything else is patience.
What good looks like: a smooth revenue ramp with capacity utilisation near 90% and no revolver draw. If you're at $8M with cash to spare, you left price on the table; if you drew the revolver, you built too much plant too early.
BM · 02

Four Dials, No Waste

Competency — demand-driver discipline
Dose
In your real worldSOV vs SOM. Every driver has a saturation point; spend past it and you're buying share of voice that no longer converts to share of market.
Setup
One live brandAny area, any start. At least 12 months post-launch.
Objective
All four ≥ 60%awareness, detailing, access, credibility — simultaneously, while cash-flow positive for 6 straight months.
Constraints
One brand's budgetNo selling other assets to subsidise it. It funds itself.
Moves to run
  1. Push each driver up one at a time and watch where extra spend stops moving the meter — that's its saturation point for this area.
  2. Rebalance toward the drivers your area rewards: credibility and access in specialty; detailing and access in primary care.
  3. Once all four clear 60%, trim to maintenance — the decay rate, not the build rate. Redeploy the saved cash.
The lesson
The skill isn't spending — it's knowing the exact point where a driver stops paying you back, and stopping there.
What good looks like: four meters held in a tight band on a budget that shrinks after month three. If any driver needs constant increases to stay up, you're funding decay, not growth.
BM · 03

The Access Trade

Competency — market access & gross-to-net
Dose
In your real worldThe lead-payer rebate demand. Say yes and you win the tier but shrink net price everywhere; say no and you protect price but lose covered lives. The trap is buying access that doesn't convert.
Setup
Immunology brand(or any brand when the payer-rebate event fires). Biologic economics make this bite hardest.
Objective
Grow share, GtN < 30%Increase covered volume while keeping gross-to-net under roughly 30%.
Constraints
No price rescueYou can't raise list price to paper over the rebate. Access must earn its cost.
Moves to run
  1. Before accepting any rebate, check the share it actually buys against the net-price it costs in the portfolio view.
  2. Accept access that moves volume; hold price and lose the tier when the rebate buys coverage you can't convert.
  3. Use medical affairs to lift credibility — it grows share without touching gross-to-net at all.
The lesson
Access costs price. Coverage you can't convert into prescriptions is margin you handed a payer for nothing.
What good looks like: rising share with gross-to-net flat or falling. If share climbs but GtN blows past 30%, you bought lives you aren't converting.
BM · 04

Hold the Line

Competency — competitive response
Dose
In your real worldA competitor launches into your category. The reflex is to cut price. The disciplined answer is to out-detail and out-evidence them, and defend price.
Setup
Play to a rival launchAdvance until a competitor launches in your area (intensity jumps; the feed announces it).
Objective
Keep > 50% of peakHold your brand's market share above half its pre-launch peak through the next 12 months.
Constraints
No price warDefend without cutting price more than 5%. Win on reach and credibility.
Moves to run
  1. Respond with detailing and medical affairs, not discounts — reach and credibility are what a new entrant can't buy quickly.
  2. Reinforce access so the rival can't take your formulary position while you're distracted.
  3. Accept a lower plateau if you must — the goal is a defended floor, not the old peak.
The lesson
A share you aren't defending is a share you're donating. Price is the lazy lever; reach and evidence are the durable ones.
What good looks like: share dips, then stabilises above the 50% line on a defended net price. If you held share only by slashing price, you won the battle and lost the P&L.
Tier II

Director

Run a portfolio. Decide where to invest, where to stop, and how to survive the cliff.
DIR · 05

Portfolio Triage

Competency — prioritisation & resource allocation
Dose
In your real worldThree brands, one budget. The hardest call a director makes isn't where to invest — it's where to stop. Harvesting a declining brand to fund a growth one is the whole job.
Setup
3+ marketed brandsBuild to a portfolio with at least one asset past its growth phase or nearing LOE.
Objective
Reach Grade BEnterprise value ≥ $600M while deliberately harvesting a declining brand — promo cut to maintenance, cash redeployed to a growth asset.
Constraints
No new capitalFund growth by reallocating from the harvest, not by raising money.
Moves to run
  1. Rank brands by trajectory, not size: months of exclusivity left × current growth. The oldest large brand is often the harvest candidate.
  2. On the harvest brand, cut promotion to maintenance only and let it coast — you're milking cash flow, not defending share.
  3. Redeploy that budget into the growth asset's drivers and capacity. Watch enterprise value follow the reallocation.
The lesson
Harvest, don't invest, on the brand whose franchise is over. Refusing to name it is how directors quietly starve their winners.
What good looks like: total EV rising even as one brand's revenue declines on purpose. If every brand still gets full support, you haven't triaged — you've spread the budget thin and called it fairness.
DIR · 06

The LOE Cliff

Competency — lifecycle management
Dose
In your real worldLoss of exclusivity. Generics collapse net price overnight. The revenue on the P&L the year after the cliff was decided by a pipeline choice you made half a decade earlier.
Setup
Own a brand near LOETrack a brand into the last ~24 months of exclusivity (the Market screen's clock).
Objective
< 30% revenue fallAcross the 24 months around the cliff, keep total company net revenue from dropping more than 30%.
Constraints
Replacement must be organicThe successor has to come from your own pipeline, launched before the cliff — no buying revenue to fake it.
Moves to run
  1. Count backward: a successor needs years of development. Start or accelerate a program the moment you see the cliff coming.
  2. Use accelerated trial intensity on the successor if the clock is tight and cash allows — you're buying time you don't have.
  3. Launch the replacement before exclusivity ends so its ramp overlaps the incumbent's decline.
The lesson
Plan the successor before the patent runs out. Lifecycle management is arithmetic done early, not heroics done late.
What good looks like: the incumbent's revenue falls off the cliff while a new brand's ramp catches most of it — a visible baton pass in the finance chart. If you started the successor after the feed warned you, you started too late.
DIR · 07

The Supply Bet

Competency — supply & demand planning
Dose
In your real worldThe most capital-intensive fight in the industry. In diabetes/obesity and vaccines, supply is the strategy — every unserved patient is a patient your competitor keeps forever.
Setup
Metab or VaccinesChoose a high-volume, capacity-constrained area and launch into it.
Objective
≥ 12% shareReach double-digit market share while holding capacity utilisation between 80–100% for 12 straight months.
Constraints
No chronic shortfallSustained unserved demand fails the case — that's share you're donating.
Moves to run
  1. Build capacity ahead of the ramp, not behind it — expansions take a month to arrive, and demand you miss is gone.
  2. Track the portfolio's capacity-use meter. Below 80%, you've over-built; at 100% with unmet demand, you're leaking share.
  3. Stage expansions in steps that keep utilisation in the band as demand climbs, rather than one giant idle plant.
The lesson
Capacity is a bet you place before you know the demand. Under-build and the competitor keeps your patients; over-build and your cash sits idle.
What good looks like: utilisation hugging 90% as share climbs — you're serving the market without financing empty lines. Persistent shortfall warnings in the feed mean you bet too small.

CEO

CEO · 08

The IPO Decision

Competency — capital allocation
Dose
In your real worldEquity looks free — it's cash with no interest and no repayment. It isn't free. You pay in upside, permanently, and you only find out the price years later.
Setup
Grow past $350M EVCross the IPO threshold (month 12+), then take the company public.
Objective
$600M stake, ≥ 65% ownedFinish with founder stake ≥ $600M while still holding at least 65% of the company.
Constraints
Float with a purposeEvery share you sell must fund pipeline that returns more than the dilution costs you.
Moves to run
  1. Float the smallest slice that funds your next wave of programs — a 20% float keeps 80%; resist the temptation to raise more "just in case".
  2. Put the raised cash straight into Phase III assets and capacity, where it compounds into enterprise value the market will reprice upward.
  3. If the stock runs ahead of fundamentals, a modest buyback later reclaims ownership you gave up cheaply.
The lesson
Raise equity only when the pipeline turns the cash into more than the dilution costs you. Otherwise you're shrinking your own stake to feel funded.
What good looks like: cash raised, EV climbs faster than shares issued, ownership stays high. If your market cap doubled but your stake barely moved, you funded someone else's return.
CEO · 09

The Compliance Gauntlet

Competency — risk & governance
Dose
In your real worldMLR and the promotional code. Aggressive promotion drifts you off-label; an audit turns that drift into a fine and a headline. Growth a single inspection can erase was never growth.
Setup
Push promotion hardRun high promotional spend across your brands — the aggressive posture that invites scrutiny.
Objective
Two clean auditsSurvive at least two audit events with zero fines, keep compliance above 70, and still grow revenue.
Constraints
Grow inside the codeNo pulling all promotion to hide — the point is aggressive and clean.
Moves to run
  1. Fund the compliance programme before you scale promotion — posture above 70 is what turns an audit from a fine into a formality.
  2. When an audit fires, cooperate and remediate rather than gambling on a minimal response with a thin posture.
  3. Keep revenue climbing through it — a clean audit while growing is the proof; a clean audit while frozen is just caution.
The lesson
Aggressive promotion and clean compliance aren't opposites. Fund the posture first, and the growth stops being fragile.
What good looks like: compliance held above 70 through two inspections with no fines and revenue still rising. A fine means you outran your posture; a revenue stall means you bought safety by not competing.
CEO · 10

Category Leader

Competency — total command
Dose
In your real worldThe ten-year scorecard. Not one launch, not one brand — a decade of pipeline, defence, and capital run well enough that the whole industry measures itself against you.
Setup
Any start, full decadePlay all 120 months. Difficulty is your call — hard start earns the cleaner claim.
Objective
Grade A & top 3Finish with founder stake > $1.5B AND ranked #1–3 on the industry leaderboard.
Constraints
Both, not eitherGrade A outside the top three means the market grew faster than you. Top three at Grade B means you didn't keep enough.
Moves to run
  1. Never let the pipeline board go empty — a leader always has the next launch already in development.
  2. Defend every marketed brand's four drivers and its access; rivals compound share the moment you coast.
  3. Use capital markets with discipline — raise to fund compounding assets, buy back when the stake is cheap, and keep ownership high.
  4. Watch the leaderboard as a live scoreboard: rank is the one metric that tells you whether your good decade beat everyone else's.
The lesson
Beating the table is the long game. It demands a live pipeline, defended brands, and disciplined capital — all three, for ten years, without a lapse.
What good looks like: a decade with no empty pipeline months, no undefended brand, and a stake that grew without giving the company away. This is the game solved.
Operator's scorecard

Run the tiers in order — each competency compounds into the next. Clear all four Brand Manager cases before a director should trust you with a portfolio; clear the director tier before you carry a full decade of capital risk. A case is "cleared" when you hit its number without breaking its constraint. Miss, reload, and change one decision — the point isn't to win the reload, it's to find the single move that was quietly costing you the case.
